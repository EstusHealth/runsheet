2

What boundaries are — and what they're not

8 min0:05 – 0:13
›
🎤
Say
  • Core definition again: self-defined limits that protect your core needs & values, across every domain of life.
  • It's a modern term — only common in the last ~20–25 years — but with old philosophical roots.
  • The big reframe: a boundary is about what YOU do, not about controlling them. Think "If X, then I will Y."

🃏 Cheat card — Boundary vs. its 4 look-alikes

Valuethe "why"

The enduring principle underneath (health, honesty, family). Boundaries protect values — a bad value makes a bad boundary.

Rulecontrols others

"You may not do that." An attempt to control someone else — often violates their autonomy. Not a boundary.

Preferencenegotiable

Low-stakes, tradeable. "I'd prefer Thai tonight." Nice to have, easy to flex.

Requestasks them

"Can you pick me up?" You're asking for a behavior — they can say no.

Boundaryself-defined + your action

"If you start yelling at me, I'll leave and go home." You define the limit and your own response to it.

"A good intention is not a boundary. A boundary you can't enforce with any clarity is just a suggestion — and people treat suggestions like suggestions. Eventually they start ignoring it."— on why vague boundaries fail
💬
Ask "Drop a boundary you've tried to set — we'll sort one or two live: was it actually a rule, a request, or a real boundary?"
👀
Watch for The #1 confusion is rule vs. boundary. When someone says "I told them they can't…", gently flip it: "When you do X, I will Y."
3

Goals of improving boundaries — what you're actually aiming for

13 min0:13 – 0:26
›
🎤
Say Before the "how", get clear on the "what for". The goal isn't "have no boundary problems" — it's a handful of very tangible payoffs. This is what you're protecting towards.

🃏 Cheat card — what better boundaries give you

Off your back

Less resentment

The griping, muttering and silent scorekeeping fade — the need gets said out loud and dealt with, instead of buried and stewed on.

Protected

Energy management

Your time and attention stop leaking into things you never actually chose. Fewer draining obligations, more capacity left for what matters.

Your way

Do what suits you

More of what you'd genuinely like to do, done in the way that actually suits you — saying yes on your terms, and no without the spiral.

The sum of it

Lifestyle by design

The cumulative payoff: a life you've deliberately shaped around your values and energy — not one shaped by whoever asked last.

What "good" looks like — the continuum

🎤
Say It's a spectrum, not a personality type. Most of us are porous in some areas and rigid in others. The target is the middle — clear yet flexible.
Too open

Porous

Can't say "no", over-discloses, over-flexible, makes excuses for disrespect.

→ Enmeshment, emotional contagion, loss of self-definition.

The target

Healthy

Clear expectations, protects core values, stated assertively & consistently — and adjusts to context.

→ Less conflict, mutual respect, trust.

Too closed

Rigid

Keeps everyone at arm's length, refuses help, uses ghosting as a shield.

→ Isolation, missed intimacy & connection.

"I'm not responsible for your happiness — yet I do care about it." That "yet" is the flexibility. Clear line, room to adjust.— example of a healthy, flexible boundary
💬
Ask "Which way do you lean — more porous or more rigid? Does it flip between work and home?"
👀
Watch for Don't shame the porous crowd. And name the trap: people assume rigid = "good at boundaries." It's still a boundary problem.

The real unlock: having something to protect

🎤
Say
  • Boundaries stop feeling like conflict the moment you know what you're actually defending — a relationship, your health, your career, your family, your own sense of self. Once there's something real on the other side of the line, holding it stops being "difficult" and starts being obvious.
  • But that clarity doesn't show up on its own. It's built — through intentionality, understanding your own needs, and the communication skill to actually say it out loud. Skip any one of the three and the boundary either never forms, or it forms and can't be held.
1

Intentionality

Deliberately decide what matters enough to defend — don't wait until you're already resentful to figure it out.

→ Turns boundary-setting from reactive to chosen.

2

Understanding your needs

You can't protect what you haven't named. Get specific about what you actually need — not just what you're used to tolerating.

→ Gives the boundary a real "why" underneath it.

3

Communication skill

The clearest need in the world still needs words. Build the ability to state it calmly and hold it under pushback (see the per-domain scripts later).

→ Turns intention into something others can actually respond to.

You don't find the willpower to hold a boundary from gritted teeth — you find it from knowing exactly what you're protecting.— why "something to protect" makes boundaries easier, not harder

🧠 A neurodivergent lens — why "they should just know" breaks down

🎤
Say
  • For a lot of people — and especially many neurodivergent people (autistic, ADHD) — the reason intentionality and stating needs feels so hard comes down to theory of mind: our built-in read on how much of our inner world other people can actually see.
  • It cuts two ways. Sometimes we assume others can see the path we're on — our plan, our overload, the need building up — and we feel hurt or resentful when they "should have known" and didn't. Other times our own experience feels so obvious from the inside that it never occurs to us to say it out loud at all.
  • The reframe: no one can mind-read. Your needs, your limits, the direction you're heading — none of it is visible to anyone else until you articulate it. Saying it plainly isn't over-explaining or being "too much" — it's the only thing that makes the boundary real to another person.
The gap runs both ways — two different minds simply don't come pre-loaded with each other's context. That's not one person's failing; it's just what separate minds are. The fix is the same for everyone: say the thing out loud.— the "double empathy" reframe: mismatch, not deficit
💬
Ask "Name one thing in your life that's worth protecting enough to set a boundary around it — go."
👀
Watch for Hold the neurodivergent point as neutral and shared, not a diagnosis or a deficit — articulating needs is a skill anyone can build, and it lands hardest for people who've been told they're "too much" or "not clear." Then move into the 6 steps.

The 6 steps to setting a boundary (the method)

15 min0:26 – 0:41
›

This is the method — the 8 domains next put it to work with a concrete move and a script for each. Spend the most time on Awareness, State clearly, and Guilt.

🃏 Cheat card — the 6 steps at a glance

1

Awareness

Find where you need one.

2

State clearly

Assertive, not aggressive.

3

Consistency

Enforce it — stakes are real.

4

Anticipate pushback

Plan your response.

5

Adapt

Revise; find the mean.

6

Manage guilt

Expect it; don't obey it.

1 · Awareness — where is the boundary missing?

🎤
Say The hardest step, especially if you grew up without boundaries. Three red-flag signals:
  • Resentment — what do you keep griping about, muttering under your breath, or blaming others for?
  • Ambiguity — messy, never-discussed areas (classic: merged finances no one actually talked about).
  • Compulsion / energy drain — the thing you keep doing while thinking "why do I keep doing this?"

2 · State it clearly — assertive, not aggressive

🎤
Say Use the non-violent-communication formula. It hands them their agency while you keep yours:
"When you [do X], I feel [Y], and I need [Z]."
"It's just a repackaging of the same experience — but so much more effective than 'stop being a dick.'"— NVC framing

3 · Consistency — this is where trust is built

🎤
Say "It's almost a law of the universe: any boundary that's defined will be tested." Real boundaries have stakes — if you don't follow through, it's worth nothing. Often it takes a few enforcements before it sticks.

4 · Anticipate pushback

🎤
Say People without their own boundaries resist yours: guilt trips, picking fights, ghosting. For them it can feel easier to manipulate you than to adjust. Decide your response in advance.

5 · Adapt — the golden mean, take two

🎤
Say No boundary is perfect on the first try — you'll overshoot (too rigid) or undershoot (too porous) and revise. That's normal. Just communicate the change explicitly, and define exceptions up front ("no calls after 10pm — unless it's a family emergency").

6 · Manage the guilt

🎤
Say Expect two things: they won't like it, and you'll feel guilty. That's because you're cutting off a supply of validation they'd come to rely on. Guilt is not proof you did something wrong. You may lose a few relationships — you're still better off.
💬
Ask "Which of the 6 is hardest for you? Type the number 1–6." Read the pattern back to the room.
👀
Watch for If time is short, protect steps 1, 2, and 6. Steps 3–5 can be summarized fast.
5

The 8 domains — spot it, do it, say it

13 min0:41 – 0:54
›
🎤
Say This is the method made concrete. Boundaries live across all eight of these — most people are strong in some and nearly invisible in others. For each one: spot the flag, make one move, and have one line ready to say. Poll the room, then go deep where they need it.

🃏 Cheat card — each domain, with a move and a line

1 · Physical

Space & touch

Flag: uncomfortable touch, your space used without asking.

Do: decide your limit — and your response — before the moment comes.

Say: "I'm not a hugger — a wave works for me."

2 · Relational

People-pleasing

Flag: chronic resentment, feeling like a doormat.

Do: catch the pause before "yes" — buy time instead of auto-agreeing.

Say: "Let me check and get back to you."

3 · Emotional

Your feelings vs theirs

Flag: feeling responsible for someone's happiness.

Do: separate caring from fixing — support without owning the outcome.

Say: "I care about how you feel — and I can't fix this for you."

4 · Time

Where your hours go

Flag: burnout, "time debt", always available.

Do: put a hard edge on the day and pre-decide the exceptions.

Say: "I don't take work calls after 6 — I'll reply first thing tomorrow."

5 · Identity

Being your true self

Flag: masking, "a different person around family".

Do: pick one setting where you stop performing and hold your real preference.

Say: "That doesn't work for me anymore."

6 · Intellectual

Ideas & debate

Flag: adopting others' views to avoid conflict.

Do: choose which debates are worth your energy — opt out of the rest, out loud.

Say: "We see this differently, and I'm happy to leave it there."

7 · Digital

Devices & availability

Flag: anxiety from being always-on, 2am emails.

Do: set a device curfew and tell people your real response rhythm.

Say: "I'm a slow texter — I'll get back to you when I can."

8 · Financial

Money & obligations

Flag: merged-vs-separate fights, guilt loans.

Do: make the agreement explicit before it's needed — who pays for what.

Say: "I'm not able to lend money, but I'm happy to help you think it through."

💬
Ask "Which domain is your weakest? Type the number 1–8." Then work the top two or three live — flag, move, line.

🃏 Universal lines — work in any domain

The four that fit anywhere. Hit Copy to drop them into chat as the takeaway.

The core formula (NVC)
"When you [do X], I feel [Y]. What I need is [Z]."
The clean no
"Thanks for thinking of me — I'm not able to take this on."
The if-then boundary
"If [behavior] keeps happening, I'll [your action]."
Deflect the guilt-trip
"I understand you're disappointed — and my answer is still no."
👀
Watch for Don't march through all 8 evenly — the poll tells you where to spend time. The Do + Say lines are the real takeaway; paste the ones that land into chat.
