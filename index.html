2

What boundaries are — and what they're not

8 min0:05 – 0:13
›
🎤
Say
  • Core definition again: self-defined limits that protect your core needs & values, across every domain of life.
  • It's a modern term — only common in the last ~20–25 years — but with old philosophical roots.
  • The big reframe: a boundary is about what YOU do, not about controlling them. Think "If X, then I will Y."

🃏 Cheat card — Boundary vs. its 4 look-alikes

Valuethe "why"

The enduring principle underneath (health, honesty, family). Boundaries protect values — a bad value makes a bad boundary.

Rulecontrols others

"You may not do that." An attempt to control someone else — often violates their autonomy. Not a boundary.

Preferencenegotiable

Low-stakes, tradeable. "I'd prefer Thai tonight." Nice to have, easy to flex.

Requestasks them

"Can you pick me up?" You're asking for a behavior — they can say no.

Boundaryself-defined + your action

"If you start yelling at me, I'll leave and go home." You define the limit and your own response to it.

"A good intention is not a boundary. A boundary you can't enforce with any clarity is just a suggestion — and people treat suggestions like suggestions. Eventually they start ignoring it."— on why vague boundaries fail
💬
Ask "Drop a boundary you've tried to set — we'll sort one or two live: was it actually a rule, a request, or a real boundary?"
👀
Watch for The #1 confusion is rule vs. boundary. When someone says "I told them they can't…", gently flip it: "When you do X, I will Y."
3

The boundary continuum: porous → healthy → rigid

6 min0:13 – 0:19
›
🎤
Say It's a spectrum, not a personality type. Most of us are porous in some areas and rigid in others. The goal is the middle — clear yet flexible.

🃏 Cheat card — the three settings

Too open

Porous

Can't say "no", over-discloses, over-flexible, makes excuses for disrespect.

→ Enmeshment, emotional contagion, loss of self-definition.

The target

Healthy

Clear expectations, protects core values, stated assertively & consistently — and adjusts to context.

→ Less conflict, mutual respect, trust.

Too closed

Rigid

Keeps everyone at arm's length, refuses help, uses ghosting as a shield.

→ Isolation, missed intimacy & connection.

"I'm not responsible for your happiness — yet I do care about it." That "yet" is the flexibility. Clear line, room to adjust.— example of a healthy, flexible boundary
💬
Ask "Which way do you lean — more porous or more rigid? Does it flip between work and home?"
👀
Watch for Don't shame the porous crowd. And name the trap: people assume rigid = "good at boundaries." It's still a boundary problem.
4

Why boundaries matter (the "it's not selfish" beat)

6 min0:19 – 0:25
›
🎤
Say — keep it light, ~90 sec
  • Philosophy. Kant: true freedom is self-mastery, not indulging every craving. Aristotle: virtue is the golden mean — the balance between too much (porous) and too little (rigid).
  • Psychology. Freud: the ego forms at the boundary between self and world. Good boundaries are a lagging indicator of maturity and a healthy identity.
  • Payoff. Less conflict, better emotional regulation, more respect & trust from others, protected energy, a more authentic life.
"Boundaries are the ultimate act of love — not just for your own health and safety, but for the other person's. They'll never learn to stand on their own two feet as long as they're dependent on you."— the reframe for anyone who feels guilty
💬
Ask "One thing good boundaries would free up energy for — go."
👀
Watch for Don't over-teach the philosophy. It's a 60–90 second "here's why this isn't selfish" — then move.

The 6 steps to setting a boundary (the core)

18 min0:25 – 0:43
›

This is the heart of the session — spend the most time here, especially on Awareness, State clearly, and Guilt.

🃏 Cheat card — the 6 steps at a glance

1

Awareness

Find where you need one.

2

State clearly

Assertive, not aggressive.

3

Consistency

Enforce it — stakes are real.

4

Anticipate pushback

Plan your response.

5

Adapt

Revise; find the mean.

6

Manage guilt

Expect it; don't obey it.

1 · Awareness — where is the boundary missing?

🎤
Say The hardest step, especially if you grew up without boundaries. Three red-flag signals:
  • Resentment — what do you keep griping about, muttering under your breath, or blaming others for?
  • Ambiguity — messy, never-discussed areas (classic: merged finances no one actually talked about).
  • Compulsion / energy drain — the thing you keep doing while thinking "why do I keep doing this?"

2 · State it clearly — assertive, not aggressive

🎤
Say Use the non-violent-communication formula. It hands them their agency while you keep yours:
"When you [do X], I feel [Y], and I need [Z]."
"It's just a repackaging of the same experience — but so much more effective than 'stop being a dick.'"— NVC framing

3 · Consistency — this is where trust is built

🎤
Say "It's almost a law of the universe: any boundary that's defined will be tested." Real boundaries have stakes — if you don't follow through, it's worth nothing. Often it takes a few enforcements before it sticks.

4 · Anticipate pushback

🎤
Say People without their own boundaries resist yours: guilt trips, picking fights, ghosting. For them it can feel easier to manipulate you than to adjust. Decide your response in advance.

5 · Adapt — the golden mean, take two

🎤
Say No boundary is perfect on the first try — you'll overshoot (too rigid) or undershoot (too porous) and revise. That's normal. Just communicate the change explicitly, and define exceptions up front ("no calls after 10pm — unless it's a family emergency").

6 · Manage the guilt

🎤
Say Expect two things: they won't like it, and you'll feel guilty. That's because you're cutting off a supply of validation they'd come to rely on. Guilt is not proof you did something wrong. You may lose a few relationships — you're still better off.
💬
Ask "Which of the 6 is hardest for you? Type the number 1–6." Read the pattern back to the room.
👀
Watch for If time is short, protect steps 1, 2, and 6. Steps 3–5 can be summarized fast.
6

The 8 domains — a fast self-audit

5 min0:43 – 0:48
›
🎤
Say Boundaries live across all of these. Most people are strong in some and nearly invisible in others. Run it as a quick map, not a deep dive.

🃏 Cheat card — the 8 domains

1 · Physical

Space & touch

Flag: uncomfortable touch, your space used without asking.

Healthy: "Please don't make her hug anyone."

2 · Relational

People-pleasing

Flag: chronic resentment, feeling like a doormat.

Healthy: notice the pause before you say yes.

3 · Emotional

Your feelings vs theirs

Flag: feeling responsible for someone's happiness.

Healthy: "I care, but I'm not responsible for how you feel."

4 · Time

Where your hours go

Flag: burnout, "time debt", always available.

Healthy: "No work email after I leave the office."

5 · Identity

Being your true self

Flag: masking, "a different person around family".

Healthy: "That won't work for me anymore."

6 · Intellectual

Ideas & debate

Flag: adopting others' views to avoid conflict.

Healthy: choose which debates are worth your energy.

7 · Digital

Devices & availability

Flag: anxiety from being always-on, 2am emails.

Healthy: Do-Not-Disturb at 7pm; "I'm a slow texter."

8 · Financial

Money & obligations

Flag: merged-vs-separate fights, guilt loans.

Healthy: explicit "who pays for what" conversation.

💬
Ask "Which domain is your weakest? Type the number." Great low-stakes participation moment.
7

Why it's so hard — toxic dynamics & barriers

7 min0:48 – 0:55
›
🎤
Say Boundaries don't get set inside enmeshed / codependent relationships — because the boundary interrupts the dynamic that both people are feeding on. Picture it: one person walks room to room setting fires, the other sprints behind with a fire hose. Both get to feel needed.

🃏 Cheat card — the 3 self-reinforcing patterns

Pattern 1

Victim ↔ Savior

One is always getting hurt; the other always rescues. Feel victimized → get attention → find more reasons to be a victim.

Pattern 2

Breaker ↔ Fixer

Nothing's ever good enough; the other keeps trying to make it good enough. Unhappiness gets rewarded.

Pattern 3

Runner ↔ Chaser

One pulls away; the other chases harder to win them back. The chase itself becomes the relationship.

Set the boundary and one of two things happens: the relationship heals, or it ends — and in both cases, you're better off.— the coach's bottom line

Barriers — why it's hard for you specifically

🎤
Say (name them, don't dwell)
  • Failed individuation — never fully separated your identity from family.
  • Insecure attachment — anxious styles fear the loss, so they fold.
  • High agreeableness — pulls you porous (peace at any cost).
  • High conscientiousness — can pull you rigid / over-responsible.
  • Stress & energy depletion — willpower's gone, so the line slips.
💬
Ask "Recognize any of these patterns? No need to name names — just a yes/no in chat."
👀
Watch for Sensitive territory. Normalize, don't diagnose. Offer the reframe, then move to the practical scripts.
8

Scripts: saying no without guilt or drama

3 min + takeaway0:55 – 0:58
›
🎤
Say "You don't need the perfect words — you need a few you can reuse." Paste these into the chat as the takeaway. Hit Copy to grab any line.
The core formula (NVC)
"When you [do X], I feel [Y]. What I need is [Z]."
The clean no
"Thanks for thinking of me — I'm not able to take this on."
No, without a reason
"That doesn't work for me."
Buy the pause
"Let me check and get back to you." (protects the pause before an automatic yes)
The if-then boundary
"If [behavior] keeps happening, I'll [your action]."
Family / holidays
"That won't work for me anymore — but here's what I can do…"
Work / time
"I don't check email after 6 — I'll reply first thing tomorrow."
Money
"I'm not able to lend money, but I'm happy to help you think it through."
Deflect the guilt-trip
"I understand you're disappointed — and my answer is still no."
